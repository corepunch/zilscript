The Midnight Ledger
Studio Null, 2026
Release 1

You are the examiner the harbour auction house sends for when something goes missing, and tonight it has called you out through the rain. At the midnight weigh-in, Lot 9, a brass astrolabe, turned out to be gone from its sealed transit crate. The porter, Ives, is already being told to sign for the loss. Before anyone signs anything, the hearing wants your finding: where the astrolabe was taken, when, and by whom. Ives can refuse to sign, but his wages will be held while the accusation stands. Every detail you put before the hearing may give him a way to contest it.

Auction Vestibule
You are in the vestibule of the auction house, a draughty entrance hall with wet footprints all over the tiles. In the corner, a coffee urn has cooked down to mud, and nobody here has the authority to empty it. A damp notice is pinned to the wall. The hearing chamber is to the north, and from it comes a low murmur of voices. The cloakroom, where the porter is waiting, is to the east.

> north
This is the hearing chamber. The backs of the empty chairs shine where generations of witnesses have gripped them. Clerk Venn sits at the head of the long table, her pen poised over a blank form. The hinged evidence panel is set where everyone must look at it. The weigh room is to the west and the records room to the east. The public registry is to the north, and the vestibule is back to the south.

> west
This is the weigh room, where every crate is weighed into the auction and out again. Dust lies over the floor, except in clean squares where crates were standing. The big platform weigh scale has stopped swinging, and a paper strip hangs from its printer. A clock ticks on the wall above it. The hearing chamber is back to the east.

> examine scale
The scale prints a paper strip for every weighing. Tonight's strip shows the same crate weighed twice, six minutes apart. The second time it was exactly eleven pounds lighter: the weight of the astrolabe and its felt tray. The times on the strip are stamped by a separate pulse sent down the speaking tube, not taken from the wall clock.

> east
This is the records room, shelves of old record books under a leaking ceiling. Tonight's book, the midnight ledger, lies face up under the one dry square of ceiling. A key cabinet hangs on the wall, and it smells very faintly of oranges. Beyond an open grille to the north, the transit cage is unlit. The hearing chamber is back to the west.

> read ledger
Tonight's entries, in the scale clerk's neat hand. He copied both time stamps from the scale strip: at 11:57 the crate weighed eleven pounds more; at 12:03, the same crate weighed eleven pounds less. In the margin a bracket joins the two lines, and Ives has signed it. The bracket records two WEIGHINGS of the crate, not two visits to the cage.

> examine cabinet
Every cage key hangs on its own brass tag. Ives's key is still chained to the wall, under a printed rule that a porter must stay beside the scale during a weigh-in. The assessor's tag, Orr's, is back on its hook now, and it is smeared with the orange-scented oil used on fresh hinge pins.

> north
This is the transit cage, where sold lots wait to go out on the morning boats. The wire walls throw a net of shadows across the boards. A syndicate seal dangles crooked from a hook above a transit crate, which has its lid resting at a slant. The lit records room is back to the south.

> examine seal
A wax seal on a signed paper tag. But it is the ASSAY seal, the one fixed through the crate's inside hinge, not the dispatch seal. Ives's chalked dispatch mark is on the lid, somewhere else entirely. So the lid could be lifted from the hinge side without disturbing Ives's chalk, but not without an assessor's key.

> examine crate
The crate is empty except for two felt corners from the astrolabe's tray. On the hinge edge, newly exposed, is a half-circle of oil. Ives's chalk mark on the outside of the lid is unbroken; nobody had to touch it.

> north
This is the public registry, where everyone who comes or goes on the night shift signs the book. Rain dripping from the people who leaned over to sign has softened the pages of the shift register. Across the room, Assessor Orr, a neat, narrow man in a grey suit, keeps unbuttoning and rebuttoning one cuff, a long document sleeve clamped under his other arm. The hearing chamber is back to the south.

> read register
The night's signatures. At 11:55, Orr signs out of the registry; at 12:06, he signs back in. Beside those lines, the crossing guard has initialled Ives as present in the weigh room the whole time. And the assessor's cage key is marked OUT for that same interval. It was not a key the porter could have borrowed.

> south

> turn panel
You lay the three columns together. WHEN: the crate got eleven pounds lighter between 11:57 and 12:03. WHERE: it was opened through the assay hinge, which needn't disturb a dispatch mark. WHO: that latch needed the assessor's key, and the key was out for the whole interval. Venn slides down two paper tabs, an Ives tab and an Orr tab, each with a mark for your thumb.

> turn ives tab
You hesitate. Ives made the chalk mark, not the inner seal, so where could he have got a key? Venn leaves both tabs open and the finding unsigned. Across the table Ives releases the breath he was holding; Orr looks down at the sleeve in his lap. The evidence still needs an explanation, not a convenient name.

> turn orr tab
Orr signing out of the registry proved nothing on its own. The key, the unbroken dispatch mark and the missing eleven pounds prove enough. Without a word, Orr draws the long brass instrument out of his document sleeve and lays it on the table, as neatly as if he were entering it in a column. Ives takes his coat down from the wall at last. He does not thank you until you are both out under the same rain.
